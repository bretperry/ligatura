ligatura

Give it two books. It finds the places where one book borrows from the other: a quote, a retelling, or a quiet nod.

Nothing to install. Run it once on two files.

npx ligatura link first-book.txt second-book.txt > links.jsonl

Keep the ligatura command on your computer. Needs Node 20 or later.

npm install -g ligatura

Use it as a library in your own project.

npm install ligatura
import { loadUnits, link, toLinks } from 'ligatura'

const source = loadUnits('imitation.txt')
const target = loadUnits('gospels.jsonl')
for await (const r of link(source, target))
  for (const l of toLinks(r, target, { top: 3 }))
    console.log(l.source, '→', l.target, l.score)

Above is a real find. Thomas à Kempis wrote about knowing Jesus "in the breaking of bread". ligatura spotted that he was echoing the road to Emmaus in Luke.

It goes sentence by sentence through the first book. For each one, it lists the few spots in the second book that sound most like it, and shows why. You decide if each one is real. ligatura only points; it never decides.

For editors, it drafts the apparatus fontium: the list of sources a critical edition prints beneath the text. For readers of reception history, it surfaces candidate intertexts across a whole corpus, ranked and with their evidence, for a human to adjudicate.

Free, and it stays on your computer. No account, no sign-up, nothing sent away.

npx ligatura link imitation.jsonl gospels.jsonl > links.jsonl

How it works

  1. Same meaning. A small language model reads every sentence and turns its meaning into numbers. Sentences that mean the same thing get close numbers, even in different words.

    Dense retrieval: cosine similarity between nomic-embed-text-v1.5 sentence embeddings, with the model's query and document prefixes.

  2. Same words. It also counts rare words the two share, like bread and breaking. That catches direct quotes. Old spellings like walketh count as walk.

    Lexical overlap: tf-idf cosine over stems, with Early Modern English inflections (-eth, -est) and archaic function words (thou, hath) normalised first.

  3. Combine. It blends both lists into one ranking, so a match that does well on both rises to the top.

    Reciprocal rank fusion, k = 60: each list contributes 1 / (60 + rank).

  4. Second opinionoptional A bigger model can re-read the top 30 and re-sort them. It's off for now: in our test it made the #1 pick worse, not better.

    Pointwise reranking by P(yes) from Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B over a candidate pool of 30. See §3 below.

What you give it, what you get back

  • A plain text file works. ligatura cuts it into sentences and numbers them.
  • Or a list with your own labels, one line each, like a Bible verse: {"ref": "MAT.5.3", "text": "Blessed are the poor in spirit..."}. Then the results use your labels: verse numbers, Stephanus pages, book.chapter.sentence.

You get back one line per possible match, with the evidence:

{"source":"4.14.5","target":"LUK.24.35","rank":1,"score":0.792,"dense":0.792,"lex":0.295,
 "shared":["breaking","bread","way"],"status":"suggested",
 "method":{"embed":"nomic-embed-text-v1.5","rerank":"none"},"date":"2026-11-08"}

score is how close the meaning is, from 0 to 1. shared lists the words both passages use. You get the top 3 per sentence; change that with --top.

Every record carries its method and date, so a link can be cited, re-run, and audited. status starts as suggested; promoting it is the scholar's act, not the tool's.

Swapping the models

The defaults are fine to start. If you already run a model server, you can point ligatura at it.

StageBuilt inOr a server you run
Meaningnomic-embed-text-v1.5, bge-small-en-v1.5, bge-large-en-v1.5--embed openai:<base-url>#<model> (Ollama, LM Studio, llama.cpp, vLLM)
Second opinionOff; --rerank qwen3-reranker-0.6b--rerank api:<url>#<model>, any /rerank endpoint
Judge–--rerank chat:<base-url>#<model>, a chat model picks the links
  • The default is fast. The second opinion is slow: about half a second per pair, so a whole book takes hours.
  • Passwords for servers go in LIGATURA_API_KEY.
  • Nothing costs money unless you point it at a paid service yourself.

What it can't do yet

  • English only, for now. Greek or Latin needs a different model plugged in.
  • Nods to a whole story, like a hint at the prodigal son with none of its words, usually slip past.
  • It suggests; you check. Read both passages before you trust a match.

ligatura 0.1 · technical noteKempis and the Gospels

Finding the Gospels in The Imitation of Christ

An evaluation of ligatura 0.1 against an editor's footnotes · 2026-11-08

Abstract. We test ligatura on a known case of dense biblical reuse: Thomas à Kempis's Imitatio Christi against the four Gospels, scored against the footnotes of the printed edition. Fused dense and lexical retrieval places the footnoted verse first for 35 of 49 links and within the top ten for 44. A small pointwise reranker, added to recover allusions, lowers first-rank accuracy in every configuration we tried. ligatura 0.1 therefore ships with reranking off.

§1Materials

The source is William Benham's English translation, Books 1–4, cut into 2,432 sentences. The target is the Douay-Rheims Gospels (1899 American edition), 3,766 verses. The gold set is the edition's own Gospel footnotes: 51 links, of which 49 point at verses present in the target.1 The 44 footnoted sentences are queried against every Gospel verse.

§2Measure

Recall at k: the share of footnoted links whose verse appears among the first k suggestions. A neighbouring verse also counts, because the footnotes follow King James numbering in John 6 and sometimes cite the verse beside the one quoted.2 The measure is conservative in one direction: footnotes are not exhaustive, so a suggestion absent from them is not thereby wrong.

The q8 model is not simply a noisier copy of the full-precision one. Over 90 query–verse pairs, its P(yes) scores differ from a reference MLX run by 0.18 on average (at most 0.80); an fp32 export differs by 0.006 (at most 0.038).3 Yet even at full precision the reranker does not beat retrieval at k = 1. Quantization explains part of the loss, not all of it.

§4Discussion

The misses sort into kinds a reader of the Imitatio will recognise. One is a second footnote on a sentence whose first is found: 3.56.10 quotes Matthew 19:17 directly (found) and the editor also cites 19:21 (rank 11). Two are paraphrase under different vocabulary: honour for glory at John 5:44 (rank 31), give unto God for render at Matthew 22:21 (rank 147). The last is narrative allusion, the prodigal's return, which no sentence-level method recovers by overlap of meaning or words. Widening the pool, or a judge that reads whole pericopes, are the next things to test.
